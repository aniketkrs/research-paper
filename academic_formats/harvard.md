# Harvard Style Guide

Concise style notes for Harvard-style social science / business /
humanities papers. Read together with `templates/harvard-paper.md` and
`references/citation-styles.md §1`.

## Front matter

- **Title:** Title Case, descriptive (avoid puns or marketing).
- **Authors:** "First Last" with affiliation footnotes.
- **Abstract:** 150–250 words. Structure (often explicit subheadings):
  Background, Aim, Method, Findings, Implications.
- **Keywords:** 5–8, indexable.

## Sectioning

- **Arabic numerals** (1, 1.1, 1.1.1).
- Section titles in Title Case.
- Conventional section order:
  1. Introduction
  2. Theoretical / Conceptual Framework
  3. Literature Review
  4. Research Questions / Hypotheses
  5. Methodology
  6. Findings / Analysis
  7. Discussion
  8. Implications
  9. Limitations
  10. Conclusion

## Citations

- **Harvard (author, year)** in text. Examples:
  - Single author: "(Smith, 2023)"
  - Two authors: "(Smith and Doe, 2023)"
  - Three+: "(Smith et al., 2023)"
  - Page-specific: "(Smith, 2023, p. 45)"
  - Multiple sources: "(Smith, 2023; Doe, 2022)"
  - Author as part of sentence: "Smith (2023) argued that …"

- Reference list **alphabetical** by family name; **hanging indent**.

- Reference example (journal article):
  > Smith, J. A., Doe, A. and Lee, K. B. (2023) 'Large language models in
  > software engineering: A systematic survey', *ACM Computing Surveys*,
  > 55(4), pp. 1–37. doi:10.1145/3589334.

- Reference example (book):
  > Smith, J. A. (2021) *Title of the Book*. 3rd edn. Cambridge:
  > Cambridge University Press.

## Theoretical framework

- Conventional and often missing in AI-generated drafts.
- Place as §2, immediately after Introduction.
- Build on foundational theory (Author, Year) and recent extensions.
- Often accompanied by a "conceptual model" figure with constructs and
  hypothesized relationships.

## Hypotheses

- State formally as **H1, H2, H3** in §4.
- Each hypothesis: directional, with rationale citation, and operational
  definition.
- Test each in the Findings section.

## Reliability and validity

- For surveys: report Cronbach's α (or ω) for every multi-item construct;
  report CFA fit indices (CFI, TLI, RMSEA, SRMR) when applicable.
- Report **discriminant validity** (Fornell-Larcker, HTMT) for SEM.
- Report **common-method bias** remedies: Harman's single-factor,
  marker-variable, or post-hoc CFA.

## Researcher positionality (qualitative)

- For qualitative or mixed-methods, declare positionality and reflexivity
  in the Methodology section.
- Discuss how positionality may have shaped data collection and
  interpretation.

## Implications

- **Theoretical implications:** how does this advance the framework?
- **Practical / managerial implications:** what should practitioners do
  differently?
- **Policy implications** (when relevant): how should policy change?

These three are often expected as separate subsections in business /
education / policy-oriented venues.

## Numbers and tense

- Spell out integers ≤ 9 in prose; digits for ≥ 10.
- Past tense for methods and findings; present for theory and
  implications.
- "Participants completed the survey" (past); "These findings suggest
  that …" (present).

## Length

- Journal articles: 6,000–10,000 words typical.
- Conference proceedings: 4,000–8,000.
- Some social-science journals are word-budgeted to ≤ 8,000.

## Submission hygiene

- Anonymize for double-blind (most social-science journals are
  double-blind).
- Include conflicts of interest, funding, ethics approval references.
- Cover letter highlighting fit with the journal's scope.

## British vs. American English

- Pick one and stay consistent. Many Harvard-tradition journals are UK-
  based (Routledge, SAGE) and prefer British spellings (analyse,
  organisation, behaviour).
